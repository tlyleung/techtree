Cognitive enhancement means making healthy people lastingly smarter: better at learning, reasoning and remembering across the board, not just more alert for an afternoon. A safe way to do it would be one of the most consequential technologies imaginable, speeding up science and helping people of all abilities, though it also raises hard questions of fairness and consent.

There are promising hints. Mice engineered to overexpress a memory-related receptor in the forebrain learn faster, and an experimental hippocampal implant improved memory in epilepsy patients by about a third in laboratory tests. But the evidence in healthy people is thin. Drugs such as modafinil give modest gains on some tasks, computerised brain training improves only the tasks practised, and large genetic studies show that intelligence is shaped by hundreds of genes, each with a small effect, with no single switch to turn. No intervention has yet raised general cognitive ability in a lasting way, and a convincing large trial in healthy adults is probably decades away.
