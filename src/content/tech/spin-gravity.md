Spin gravity is the oldest idea for living comfortably in space: turn a habitat like a wheel, and the people inside are pressed against its outer floor as if by gravity. Long stays in weightlessness weaken bones, muscles and eyesight, so a rotating station could keep crews healthy on long missions and make space somewhere people could live for years, even raise families. The physics is simple; the engineering is not. A habitat has to be large, so that its turning doesn't make people dizzy, and strong, which makes it heavy to launch. Small tests work. Gemini XI made a faint artificial gravity by spinning on a tether in 1966, and in 2016 a Japanese centrifuge on the International Space Station kept mice at Earth gravity for five weeks, sparing their bones and muscles. The startup Vast has said it aims to build a spinning station, after its first, simpler modules. No person has yet lived under spin gravity in space.
