Zero-carbon flight means airliners that burn or convert hydrogen instead of kerosene, emitting water rather than carbon dioxide. Aviation is among the hardest sectors to clean up, because batteries are too heavy for long flights. A 100-seat hydrogen airliner in service would show that the short- and medium-haul routes behind much of flying's emissions could run without fossil fuel.

The idea is not new: a Soviet airliner flew with one engine on liquid hydrogen in 1988. More recently, Rolls-Royce ran an aero engine on hydrogen in 2022, in 2023 two start-ups flew regional aircraft with one propeller driven by hydrogen fuel cells, and a small German demonstrator flew for hours on liquid hydrogen.

Larger aircraft are a different matter. Liquid hydrogen must be stored at -253°C in bulky tanks, airports need new fuel supplies, and green hydrogen remains scarce and expensive. Airbus has postponed its hydrogen airliner from 2035, and one pioneering start-up has already folded. Sustainable aviation fuels are likely to carry most of the load for decades.
