Cryonic revival means bringing back a person who was cooled to cryogenic temperatures after legal death, in the hope that future medicine could repair whatever killed them and the damage done by preservation itself. The first person was preserved this way in 1967. If revival worked, death from today's incurable diseases would become, for some, a long pause rather than an end, and research towards it could also give medicine banked organs and suspended animation along the way.

The science so far is promising but partial. Small animals and isolated organs survive vitrification, in which tissue is cooled into a glass without ice: rat kidneys have been stored for months, rewarmed and transplanted, and worms have kept a learned behaviour through the process. Brain structure can be preserved in fine detail, though the best method uses a fixative that rules out simple rewarming. No mammal has yet been revived from cryogenic temperatures, and patients preserved hours after death carry damage that only very advanced repair technology could undo.
