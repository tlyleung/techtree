An anti-ageing drug would treat ageing itself rather than one disease at a time. Because age is the biggest risk factor for cancer, heart disease and dementia, slowing the underlying biology even a little could delay many illnesses together and add healthy years to life.

Animal results are encouraging. Rapamycin, a drug already used in transplant medicine, extended the lives of mice in 2009, and in 2016 briefly switching on cell-reprogramming genes reversed signs of ageing in mice. Turning that into human medicine is harder: people live long, so lifespan trials take decades, and regulators don't recognise ageing as a disease to treat. An early rapamycin-like drug improved older people's vaccine responses in 2014 but failed its phase 3 trial in 2019. The nearest milestones are in animals and narrow human uses: a lifespan drug for senior dogs is close to conditional FDA approval, and in 2026 one of the first partial-reprogramming gene therapies entered a human trial, for eye disease.
