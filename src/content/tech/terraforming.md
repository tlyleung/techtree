Terraforming means deliberately changing a whole planet's climate to make it more like Earth's. Mars is the usual candidate: it has ice, but its air is about 0.6% as thick as ours and far too cold for liquid water to last. Warming even part of it so that water could pool and flow would be the first step towards a second living world, and the largest engineering project ever attempted.

Carl Sagan proposed planetary engineering in 1961, and the idea has had serious study since McKay, Toon and Kasting's 1991 paper. The obstacles are real: a 2018 study found Mars hasn't enough accessible carbon dioxide to thicken its own atmosphere. But newer ideas are more promising. A 2024 model suggests tiny engineered rods made from Martian materials could warm the planet by about 30 °C, if they could be made at scale and released continuously for decades, and a 2019 experiment showed that sheets of aerogel could keep patches of ground above freezing. None has been tried on Mars. All would need an industrial presence there first, and planetary-protection concerns about any native Martian life could slow them further.
