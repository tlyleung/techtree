A light sail is a spacecraft that rides on light instead of fuel: photons bouncing off a large, very thin mirror give it a small, steady push. Sunlight is enough to tour the solar system, but reaching another star within a human lifetime needs far more, and the best-studied plan is a ground-based laser array pushing gram-scale probes on ultra-thin sails to a fifth of the speed of light. Such a probe could reach Alpha Centauri in about 20 years and send back the first close-up look at planets around another star.

Sails do work: Japan's IKAROS sailed on sunlight in 2010, and in 2025 a Caltech team measured laser force on a sail membrane only 50 nanometres thick. But the hard parts of an interstellar mission, including a laser array of around 100 gigawatts, a sail that survives that beam, and sending data home across four light-years, remain on paper. Breakthrough Starshot, launched with fanfare in 2016, appears to be on indefinite hold; an estimated $4.5 million was spent, and the pledged $100 million never materialised. For now, the first launch toward another star looks like a project for the second half of this century.
