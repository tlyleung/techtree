A hyperloop moves pods through a sealed tube pumped down to low air pressure, so that with little drag and magnetic levitation they could travel at airliner speeds over land, using electricity instead of jet fuel. If it worked at scale it would make city pairs a few hundred kilometres apart feel like neighbouring suburbs.

Elon Musk's 2013 Hyperloop Alpha paper set off a wave of start-ups, and the basic parts do work: pods have levitated in near-vacuum tubes, and in 2020 two people rode one at 172 km/h. But most test tracks are under a kilometre long, and China's longest, reported to have passed 623 km/h without passengers, is 2 km. The hard problems, such as building and keeping hundreds of kilometres of tube airtight, safety, and cost per kilometre, are untested. Hyperloop One, the best-funded company, shut down in 2023. Work continues at smaller scale, including a European test centre opened in 2024, and forecasters put the first commercial line decades away, if it comes at all.
