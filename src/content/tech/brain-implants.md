A brain implant reads signals from neurons and turns them into actions: moving a cursor, typing, or speaking through a synthetic voice. For people paralysed by stroke, spinal injury or ALS, it could restore the ability to talk with family at nearly normal speed. In the longer run, the same technology could restore sight and touch, or link healthy brains directly to computers.

The science is ahead of the market. Since BrainGate's first cursor control in 2006, research implants have gone from a few words a minute to decoding attempted speech at up to 78 words a minute, and one man with ALS used his implant at home for months at over 97% accuracy. What hasn't happened is approval: these are trials with a handful of participants. China's 2026 approval of Neuracle's NEO for hand movement is the first commercial approval of an invasive brain-computer interface; an approved device for speech or typing is the next threshold.
