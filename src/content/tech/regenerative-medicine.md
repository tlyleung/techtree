Regenerative medicine aims to make the body rebuild what it has lost rather than replacing it with implants or donor tissue. This page tracks the most demanding version: a drug or therapy that makes a patient grow back a whole structure, such as a tooth or new hair follicles, in its natural place. Mice, and probably humans, keep dormant tooth buds that the protein USAG-1 normally holds back. Mice lacking teeth grow them when USAG-1 is blocked, and wounded mouse skin can grow brand-new hair follicles. The tooth antibody is now in humans: a Japanese phase 1 trial finished without serious safety problems, and a phase 2 trial in people born without six or more adult teeth is planned. If it works, it would offer a living alternative to dentures and implants. Neighbouring approaches are moving fast too, including stem-cell islets that freed most patients in an early trial from insulin injections and lab-grown red blood cells already given to volunteers, though these are transplants rather than regrowth.
