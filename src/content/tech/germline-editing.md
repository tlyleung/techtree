Germline editing means correcting a disease-causing gene in an embryo, so the child is born without the condition and does not pass it on. For families carrying severe inherited disorders, it could offer a route to healthy children that embryo screening alone can't always give, for instance when both parents carry the faulty gene.

The science has advanced quickly. Embryos were first edited in the lab in 2015, and in 2017 a team corrected a mutation behind inherited heart disease. But in 2018 He Jiankui edited embryos without approval or sound medical reason, leading to three births and a prison sentence, and a scientific consensus that the technique was not ready. In 2023 the international summit on genome editing still called heritable editing unacceptable at this time. A licensed precedent does exist: the UK's mitochondrial donation programme reported eight births in 2025, though it swaps mitochondria rather than editing genes.

New startups are now testing editing tools on embryo-derived cells, aiming to show precise, safe edits. Any first birth would also need a country to permit and license it, so law and public trust matter as much as technology.
