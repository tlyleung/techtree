An ancestor simulation is a computer model of a whole society detailed enough that the people in it have minds of their own. Nick Bostrom gave the idea its name in 2003, in an argument that if such simulations are ever run in large numbers, people today may well be living in one. Built openly, the same technology could let historians replay the past, let researchers test policies on whole simulated societies, and offer uploaded minds a place to live.

Today the pieces are small and far apart. Language-model agents can fill a simulated town or a Minecraft society, mimic the survey answers of real people fairly well, or number a million on a mock social network, but none has a human-level mind. The most complete brain simulation is of a fruit fly, with about 125,000 neurons, a tiny fraction of a human brain. Running a million human minds for a lifetime would need both working mind emulation and vast, efficient computers, and it raises hard ethical questions about creating beings who suffer.
