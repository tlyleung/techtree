A general-purpose humanoid robot has a body shaped like ours, so in principle it can work in spaces built for people: walking through warehouses, using ordinary tools and, one day, helping at home. The bet behind today's wave of companies is that machine learning will let one robot design learn many tasks, and that mass production will bring the price down to that of a car or less.

For decades humanoids were research showpieces, from Honda's ASIMO onward. That changed in the mid-2020s. Agility's Digit moves totes in paid warehouse work, Figure says its robots ran daily on a BMW assembly line for about six months, and Chinese makers such as AgiBot and Unitree now ship tens of thousands a year. Most deployments are still pilots, most robots shipped so far are used for entertainment, research or training data rather than work, and some demonstrations rely on remote operators. A million robots would mark the point where humanoids stop being prototypes and become a mass-produced product, with forecasters on Manifold giving better-than-even odds of reaching it before 2030.
