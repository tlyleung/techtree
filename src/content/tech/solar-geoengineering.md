Solar geoengineering means cooling the planet by reflecting a little more sunlight back to space, most often by spraying fine sulphate or other particles into the stratosphere, where they would linger for a year or two. Nature has run the experiment: the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo cooled the world by about half a degree for two years. A deliberate version could, in principle, take the edge off the worst heat while emissions are cut, buying time for the slower work of decarbonising. It would not remove carbon dioxide, and it carries real risks, from shifting rainfall to a sudden rebound in warming if stopped. That makes it one of the most contested ideas in climate policy. Research is still small and early. Only a few tiny balloon releases have been attempted, one by a start-up that sought no approvals, and the UK began funding small, reversible outdoor experiments in 2025. A start-up, Stardust, has raised $60 million to build a delivery system, but no one has yet put aerosols into the stratosphere at a scale that could change the climate.
