A preventive HIV vaccine would teach the immune system to block the virus before it can take hold. More than forty years into the epidemic there is still none, and finding one would protect people who can't take daily or injected preventive drugs, and could help end new infections worldwide.

HIV is hard to vaccinate against because it mutates rapidly and hides its vulnerable spots. Conventional vaccines have repeatedly failed: the best result, Thailand's RV144 trial in 2009, cut infections by only about 31%, and the large Mosaico trial was stopped in 2023 for no effect.

Researchers now aim to coax the body into making rare, broadly neutralising antibodies through a carefully designed series of shots. Human trials have shown early signals: in 2024 one vaccine induced antibodies that neutralise some HIV strains, and in 2025 another primed the right antibody precursors in most recipients. In 2026 a full sequence of shots produced broadly neutralising antibodies in almost half of vaccinated macaques. The regimen is still too long for practical use, and it must be simplified and proven in people before any efficacy trial can begin.
